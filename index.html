Quick guide to what can wait

Get help before moving the car. A flat, a bulge, visible cords, fast air loss, or a hard wobble after wheel service can mean serious damage. Arrange a spare or tow from a safe place.

Call for an inspection. Report a tire that keeps losing air, a nail, new vibration, uneven wear, or a light that flashes then stays on. Ask how to get the car there.

Check at home. After a cold snap, check each tire with a gauge and use the door label. If it loses air again or the light stays on, call the shop.

Roadside help

A passenger can read this. If you’re driving, keep your attention on the road.

Immediate danger or stuck in a traffic lane?

Turn on your hazard lights if you can. Call 911 in the U.S. Tell the dispatcher your road, direction, and nearest exit or mile marker. Follow their directions.

  1. A tire blows while you’re moving

    Hold the wheel firmly with both hands. Keep the car steady. Avoid hard braking or a sudden lift off the gas. Ease off the gas gradually. Once you have control, slow down and pull off where it is safe.

  2. You’re stopped near moving traffic

    Keep clear of traffic. Avoid changing a tire on an exposed shoulder. If exiting means stepping into traffic, stay belted inside and call for help. If the car itself poses an immediate danger, move to safety if you can and follow the dispatcher’s directions.

  3. The car is parked in a safe place

    A flat tire, an outward bulge, exposed cords, or a tire that quickly loses air needs help before the car moves. Use roadside service or arrange a tow. Tell them if you have a usable spare or a wheel-lock key.

What to say

“I’m on [road], headed [direction], near [exit or landmark]. The car is [in a lane / on the shoulder / in a parking lot]. I have [a flat / a damaged tire]. I have [a spare / a kit / no spare / I’m unsure].”

Find the right pressure

A gauge measures air pressure in pounds per square inch (psi). Check your tires once a month and before a long trip.

  1. Open the driver’s door

    Look on the frame or door edge for the tire and loading label. Find the cold psi for the front and rear tires. They can be different. If the label is missing, check the owner’s manual or ask the vehicle maker. The maximum on the tire is a different number.

  2. Check when the tires are cold

    A car parked for at least three hours gives a cold reading, even on a warm day. Remove the valve cap. Press a gauge squarely onto the valve. If it keeps hissing, lift it and try again. Read and write down the psi.

  3. Add air and check again

    For a sound tire that is a little low, follow the pump instructions. Add air in short bursts or set the pump to the correct target. Take a fresh gauge reading and put the cap back on. Check all four tires and any inflatable spare. The spare has its own target.

What if I drove to the pump?

A warm tire often reads higher. Leave the air in if driving raised the reading. Check again cold. If a warm tire is low, intact, and holding air, NHTSA advises filling it to the door label’s cold target as a temporary step. Check and adjust again when cold. If the tire went flat, leaks fast, or looks damaged, get help before driving on it.

Packing for a trip?

The car’s label gives a combined limit for people and cargo. On a practice car with a 900 lb limit, 600 lb of people leaves 300 lb for bags. If the load is too heavy, remove weight. Adding air cannot raise the car’s limit. Check the manual for your vehicle’s load instructions.

TREAD NOTES FIELD GUIDE

Check your tread

Tread is the grooved rubber that grips the road. A quick look and a simple depth gauge can tell you when to call for help.

  1. Park safely and look at each tire

    Look for an outward bubble, a deep cut, exposed cords, or tread coming loose. Get help before driving on a damaged tire. If you cannot see the inner edge safely, ask a shop to check it.

  2. Measure several spots

    Set a tread-depth gauge across a main groove. Press its tip to the bottom, away from the raised wear bar. Read the depth in 32nds of an inch. Check across the width and at several spots around the tire.

  3. Act on the most worn spot

    At 2/32 inch, or when the tread is level with a wear bar, the tire needs replacement. More tread still does not guarantee grip in rain or snow. Slow down and leave more following distance on wet roads.

TREAD PRACTICE

Which reading matters?

One edge is wearing faster

Ask the shop to measure the inner, middle, and outer tread. Uneven wear can have several causes. Ask what they found before approving alignment or other work.

Read your sidewall

The door label tells you which size your car takes. The side of the tire tells you what’s installed. Try its size or the date at the end of its DOT tire ID below.

Use a typical passenger-car size, with the letters at the end if you see them.

Enter just the last four digits of the full DOT tire ID.

I can’t find the date

The final digits can face inward. Ask the shop to read the full number for you. Stay out from under the car. The code gives a build date. Age, use, storage, damage, and the maker’s replacement guidance all matter, even on a spare.

Buying a tire?

The code describes the tire you have. Check the car’s door label and manual for approved sizes and ratings. Ask the shop about changed wheels or a different size.

Buy tires with confidence

Take a photo of the door label and bring your vehicle’s year, make, model, and trim. Tell the shop what your roads are like.

  1. Start with what fits

    Ask for the approved size, load index, and speed rating for your vehicle. This matters on EVs and light pickups too. If your front and rear sizes differ, ask how that changes the choices.

  2. Match the tire to your driving

    Tell the shop about rain, snow, ice, heat, or rough roads you drive in. All-season tires suit a range of conditions. Winter tires can give better grip in snow and cold. Summer tires are for warmer conditions. Ask what the proposed tire does well in your weather.

  3. Find out how many you need

    Ask for the tread depth on each tire and why the shop recommends one, two, or four. Some all-wheel-drive cars need closely matched tires. When the vehicle allows two matching new tires, the deeper-tread pair generally goes on the rear to help keep the car stable on wet roads.

  4. Compare the whole quote

    Ask for the installed total in writing. Have the shop list mounting, balancing, valves or sensors, disposal, and taxes. Ask which extras are optional, what the warranty covers, and what records you need to keep.

Can this puncture be repaired?

A trained shop needs to remove the tire and inspect the inside. Only certain small punctures in the main tread may qualify. A sidewall or shoulder injury is outside that area. An accepted repair fills the hole and seals the inside. A plug inserted from outside alone is incomplete.

Rotation, balance, and alignment

Rotation moves wheels between spots on the car so tread wears evenly. Follow the vehicle’s schedule and allowed pattern. Balance corrects uneven weight around a wheel and tire. Alignment adjusts wheel angles. Ask what the shop found that points to the service.

At pickup and afterward

Ask what cold pressures to use, when to rotate, and whether this vehicle needs a wheel-fastener recheck. Get the wheel-lock key back. Register new tires with the tire maker so they can reach you about a recall. You can also search the public tire recall list.

Driving a Washington mountain pass?

Look up posted restrictions before leaving. Check the car manual for compatible chains or approved alternatives. All-wheel drive can still be subject to chain rules.
